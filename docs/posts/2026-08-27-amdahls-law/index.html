Amdahl’s Law: The Speedup You Cannot Exceed

A practical guide

Performance
A reference post defining Amdahl’s law in words and mathematics.
Author

Brian D. Taylor

Published

August 27, 2026

Welcome to Infinite Limit. This is first in a series where we ask a single question asked over and over in different settings: what happens as \(N\) becomes very large? It seemed fitting to start with Amdahl’s law.

NoteTLDR

Amdahl’s law says that the speedup of an entire workload is limited by the fraction of its original running time that an improvement can actually reduce. Before accepting a system claim, ask for two numbers: the fraction of baseline wall time the improvement touches, and its end-to-end speedup.

Introduction

I felt a review of Amdahl’s law was an appropriate first post for Infinite Limit as it represents the spirit of what I’m trying to achieve on this platform; explore how things scale as \(N\) becomes very large. While not exclusively limited to photonics, it’s common that photonics people often start with a component improvement and make claims about how that improvement improves the overall system. This is also true of other components in the stack: network becomes four times faster, kernel becomes ten times faster, a section of code is spread across hundreds of processors. However what’s often missing is not how much faster that component becomes, it is how much of the original wall clock time belonged to that component in the first place.

Amdahl’s law is the accounting rule that connects those two quantities. It prevents a local speedup from being reported as though the whole workload received the same benefit. Before we begin, let’s define what wall clock time is.

Wall-clock time

Wall-clock time is defined as the actual elapsed time between the defined start and completion of a workload, as measured by an external (wall) clock:

\[ T_{\mathrm{wall}} = t_{\mathrm{finish}} - t_{\mathrm{start}}. \tag{1}\]

Wall-clock time includes every activity that delays completion: computation, communication, memory stalls, synchronization, queueing, data movement, software overhead, and idle time. Work performed concurrently by multiple processors is not added together. It is counted only through its effect on the elapsed completion time.

This distinction is essential when applying Amdahl’s law because it predicts change in total elapsed time and not in units of work or resource consumption. FLOP counts and bytes transferred measure amounts of work and not the time that work delays completion. The affected fraction \(f\) must be a fraction of baseline wall-clock time on the workload’s critical path. It cannot be inferred directly from FLOP counts, bytes transferred, aggregate processor time, or communication service time.

Amdahl’s law in words

Now that we have a definition of what wall clock time means, we need to divide the original execution time into two parts:

  1. the part affected by the proposed improvement; and

  2. everything the improvement leaves unchanged.

The affected part can shrink. The unaffected part remains. Even if the affected part were removed entirely, reduced not merely in duration but to zero, the unaffected work would still be there. This surviving work places a hard ceiling on total possible speedup from this improvement.

This may seem like a subtle point but the word affected is important here. For example, if communication overlaps useful computation, only the communication time exposed on the critical path belongs in the affected fraction: bytes moved, link utilization, or aggregate service time do not automatically equal removable wall time.

Amdahl’s law in mathematical form

Let \(f\) be the fraction of the original wall time affected by an improvement, and let \(s\) be the speedup of that affected part. The remaining fraction, \(1-f\), is unchanged. The new running time is

\[ T_1 = T_0\left[(1-f) + \frac{f}{s}\right]. \tag{2}\]

The total speedup \(S\) is simply the ratio of \(T_0\) to \(T_1\) and therefore

\[ S \equiv \frac{T_0}{T_1} = \frac{1}{(1-f) + f/s}. \tag{3}\]

This is the familiar modern statement of Amdahl’s law you can find in any textbook. Now that we have this general formula, we can parallelize it by substituting for \(f\) and \(s\). If \(p\) is the parallelizable fraction and \(N\) processors accelerate that part ideally, then

\[ S(N) = \frac{1}{(1-p) + p/N}. \tag{4}\]

The two forms are the same model. The general form uses \(f\) for any affected component and \(s\) for its actual speedup. The parallel form sets \(f = p\) and \(s = N\).

It is worth being explicit about what Equation 4 assumes, because the assumption is generous. Setting \(s = N\) says that \(N\) processors deliver exactly \(N\) times the throughput on the parallel portion, which means coordination is free: no synchronization, no collectives, no load imbalance, no contention for shared memory or fabric. Real systems pay all of those, and the payment generally grows with \(N\) rather than staying fixed. The practical consequence is that measured speedup curves do not asymptote toward \(1/(1-p)\) from below. They rise, peak at some finite \(N\), and then decline as coordination cost overtakes the shrinking parallel work. Amdahl’s law gives the ceiling. It does not promise you get to stand near it, and for interconnect-limited workloads the gap between the two is the whole engineering problem.

The limiting case

If we want to understand what happens when our improvement becomes not merely fast but instantaneous, which is to say physically impossible rather than merely expensive, we simply take the limit as \(s \to \infty\). The term \(f/s\) approaches zero, but \(1-f\) remains:

\[ S_{\max} = \lim_{s\to\infty} S = \frac{1}{1-f}. \tag{5}\]

If we say only 20% of the original execution time can be improved, then the total workload cannot become more than \(1/(1-0.20) = 1.25\) times faster.

A worked example

Let us suppose a network operation occupies 30% of baseline wall time, so \(f = 0.30\). And let’s assume we have invented a new interconnect that makes that operation five times faster, so \(s = 5\). And we will normalize the baseline time to 1.0 for easy comparison.

\[ \frac{T_1}{T_0} = (1-0.30) + \frac{0.30}{5} = 0.70 + 0.06 = 0.76. \tag{6}\]

The workload is therefore \(1/0.76 = 1.316\) times faster, and its wall time falls by 24%. The component improved by \(5\times\), but the full workload improved by only \(1.316\times\). The change is illustrated graphically below.

\[ S_{\max} = \frac{1}{1-0.30} = 1.429. \tag{7}\]

It’s clear that improvement is negligible. What it tells us is once the affected component is fast, we can see that we have quickly reached asymptotia.

How to work backwards from a claimed result

In the hardware renaissance we are currently in, it can be difficult to separate claims that are substantiated from claims that are merely asserted. Amdahl’s law gives us a compact framework for testing how impactful a claimed system speedup can be. Solving Equation 3 for the required affected fraction gives

\[ f_{\mathrm{required}} = \frac{1 - 1/S_{\mathrm{target}}}{1 - 1/s}. \tag{8}\]

This turns a performance claim into a testable question: does the baseline workload actually contain that much wall time in the component being improved?

Why this matters for Model FLOPs Utilization

For a fixed model, accelerator count, peak throughput convention, and tokens per step, Model FLOPs Utilization, MFU, is inversely proportional to step time:

\[ \mathrm{MFU} = \frac{F_{\mathrm{model/step}}}{T_{\mathrm{step}}\, P_{\mathrm{cluster,peak}}}. \tag{9}\]

Here \(F_{\mathrm{model/step}}\) is the FLOPs the model definition requires per step and \(P_{\mathrm{cluster,peak}}\) is the aggregate peak throughput of the cluster under a stated convention. Both are constants of the comparison, which is precisely why the ratio below collapses to a statement about time.

Under those fixed comparison conditions,

\[ \frac{\mathrm{MFU}_1}{\mathrm{MFU}_0} = \frac{T_0}{T_1} = S. \tag{10}\]

Raising MFU from 40% to 60% therefore requires a \(60/40 = 1.5\times\) workload speedup. Even if the targeted overhead could be eliminated completely, at least one third of the original step must belong to that overhead:

\[ f_{\min} = 1 - \frac{1}{1.5} = \frac{1}{3}. \tag{11}\]

If the component improves by \(4\times\) rather than disappearing, the required affected share rises to 44.4%. Amdahl’s law does not prove that either fraction exists. It tells us what a training-step trace must show before the MFU claim can close.

What the law does, and does not, establish

  • It is a bound, not a forecast. The equation shows what is possible under its assumptions. It does not demonstrate that the affected fraction is present or that the proposed speedup is achievable.

  • The fractions are measured in baseline wall time. You cannot mix and match wall clock time with FLOP share, byte share, energy share, and device utilization. Those quantities measure work, not time, and the two are not interchangeable.

  • Only exposed time counts. Work that is already hidden behind useful computation is not removable from the critical path a second time.

  • The component speedup must be end to end. A \(4\times\) faster link does not imply a \(4\times\) faster collective after software, protocol, serialization, switching, queueing, and synchronization are included.

  • Bottlenecks move. Accelerating one stage can expose a different or new limit somewhere else in the system. The simple equation assumes the unchanged part stays unchanged.

  • Fix the bar. We have to measure consistently. A change in the workload, precision, batch size, processor count, or definition of useful work can invalidate a direct speedup interpretation.

  • The workload is held fixed, and that is the assumption to challenge first. The standard objection to Amdahl’s law is Gustafson’s: in practice we do not run a fixed problem on a bigger machine, we run a bigger problem. If the parallel work grows with \(N\) while the serial work stays roughly constant, then \(p\) is not a constant of the system and the ceiling \(1/(1-p)\) moves with problem size. This is a real and important effect, and it is why large-scale simulation and training have been able to use machines that Amdahl’s law, read carelessly, would have declared pointless. But it is not an escape hatch from the accounting. Gustafson’s law describes a different measurement: how much more work you can complete in a fixed time, not how much faster a given workload finishes. Both are legitimate. They answer different questions, and a claim gets to invoke exactly one of them. If a speedup number is quoted for a fixed workload, as MFU-at-fixed-model-and-step comparisons are, then the fixed-workload accounting is the one that applies, and weak scaling is not available as a defense.

The original paper

Gene M. Amdahl introduced the argument in 1967 during a debate about whether large-scale computing should rely on a powerful single processor or on many cooperating processors. His short paper emphasized that real workloads contain sequential, irregular, and coordinating work that limits the return from parallel execution (Amdahl 1967).

The original paper contained no equations. Equation 4 is the compact mathematical restatement through which the argument became widely known. The durable contribution is not the notation. It is the insistence that total speedup be calculated from the execution time that can change and the time that cannot. The complete paper is linked through its DOI in the reference below (Amdahl 1967).

The practical rule

Before accepting a system claim, ask for the fraction of baseline wall time affected and its end-to-end speedup. Put both into Equation 3. If the claimed result exceeds the answer, the system claim does not close.
